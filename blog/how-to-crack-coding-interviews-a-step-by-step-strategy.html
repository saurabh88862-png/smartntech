How to Crack Coding Interviews: A Step-by-Step Strategy

Career• Oct 28, 2026• 8 min read

What interviewers evaluate and how to communicate your solution clearly.

Solving problems at home is different from solving them in front of an interviewer. Interviews test communication and problem-solving process as much as the final code. This framework helps you perform at your best.

What interviewers evaluate

  • Problem solving: can you break down an unfamiliar problem?
  • Coding: is your code correct, clean and readable?
  • Communication: do you explain your thinking clearly?
  • Testing: do you check your solution and handle edge cases?

Step 1: Clarify the problem (2 to 3 minutes)

Restate the problem in your own words and ask questions:

  • What is the input size? Can values be negative or duplicated?
  • What should be returned if there is no valid answer?
  • Is the input sorted? Can I modify it?

Work through one small example to confirm your understanding.

Step 2: Start with a brute force approach

Briefly describe the simplest correct solution and its complexity: "The brute force checks every pair, which is O(n squared)." This shows you can always produce something that works.

Step 3: Optimize

Look for patterns:

  • Repeated lookups suggest a hash map.
  • Sorted input suggests binary search or two pointers.
  • Contiguous subarrays suggest a sliding window.
  • "Top k" suggests a heap.
  • Overlapping subproblems suggest dynamic programming.

Explain the improved approach and its time and space complexity before coding. Get the interviewer's agreement.

Step 4: Write clean code

  • Use meaningful variable names: left, right, seen rather than a, b, x.
  • Break logic into small helper functions when it helps.
  • Keep talking as you code so the interviewer can follow along.

Step 5: Test your code

Walk through your code with the example, line by line. Then test edge cases:

  • Empty input or a single element.
  • All duplicates or all negative numbers.
  • Very large values that might overflow.

Fix bugs calmly. Finding your own bug is a positive signal.

Step 6: Discuss improvements

Mention trade-offs, alternative approaches or how you would handle much larger inputs.

If you get stuck

  • Say what you are thinking: "I am considering whether sorting helps here."
  • Try a smaller example or draw a diagram.
  • Ask for a hint politely. Using hints well is far better than silence.

Behavioral questions matter too

Prepare stories using the STAR method (situation, task, action, result) for questions like:

  • Tell me about a challenging project.
  • Describe a time you disagreed with a teammate.
  • What did you learn from a failure?

Preparation checklist

  • Practice problems out loud, explaining your thinking.
  • Do mock interviews with friends or online platforms.
  • Review your own projects deeply; interviewers will ask about design choices.
  • Prepare two or three thoughtful questions to ask the interviewer at the end.

On the day

Test your setup for online interviews, keep water nearby, and remember that interviewers want you to succeed. Calm, clear communication often makes the difference between two candidates with similar skills.
